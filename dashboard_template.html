Why it matters

"Closed" does not mean "solved"

On November 11, 2026, a building partially collapsed at 2625 Bedford Avenue in Flatbush, Brooklyn. In the 8 months before, 311 received at least 4 construction-related complaints at that address. Every one was closed.

  • Mar 16 · Sidewalk blocked by construction (DOT). Closed a week later: inspector "could not find the problem".
  • Mar 18 · Work contrary to approved plans or permits (DOB). Closed 51 days later: stop work order removed.
  • Apr 17 · After-hours construction noise (DEP). Closed after review.
  • Oct 15 · Construction noise (DEP). Closed the next day: no violation observed at inspection.
  • Nov 11 · Partial building collapse.

We do not claim that 311 caused this collapse; the cause is under investigation. It shows why we look beyond "was the request closed?" to "how long did it take, compared with similar requests, and where?"

All five boroughs, analyzed by 262 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (NTAs).

Limitations:

  • Closed is not fixed. Closure dates show when a request was closed, not when the problem was solved.
  • "No access" closures are excluded from wait times and shown as their own map layer, so they cannot make a neighborhood look faster.
  • Rodent requests mostly close the same day, so their wait is not meaningful. The map focuses on heat and plumbing.
  • Buildings are identified by identical coordinates (311 locations are geocoded to the address).
  • Privacy: results are shown only at neighborhood level. No individuals are identified.
Method · GeoAI

AI learns what normal looks like. GIS shows where reality departs from it.

Raw averages can be misleading. For example, heat complaints usually need an inspection, so a neighborhood that files many of them looks slow even if it is treated fairly. So we compare each request with similar requests citywide.

1
Actual wait
= Closed date - created date
2
Expected wait
A gradient boosting model learns the citywide norm from complaint type, agency, month, weekday, hour and citywide volume, without seeing the specific location.
3
Gap
= Actual Wait - Expected Wait, summarized per neighborhood. Positive means slower than similar requests citywide.
4
Clusters
Getis-Ord Gi* hot spot analysis with false discovery rate (FDR) correction, so clusters are not just chance.
Evidence it works
On requests it never saw, the model is slightly more accurate than a simple median benchmark (median error 0.74 vs 0.80 days).
Robust to the method
The model and the simple benchmark agree on 9 of the 10 slowest neighborhoods. East Harlem (North) ranks 3rd of 197 under both.
What the model learned
Complaint type explains almost all predictable wait time; timing and citywide volume add little. What remains is tied to place, which is what the map shows.
Limitations of the method
  • The benchmark does not know building age, size or owner, so part of a gap may reflect harder buildings rather than slower service.
  • Overlap between slow areas and demographics does not mean demographics cause delays (ecological fallacy).
  • Closure time is an imperfect measure of service, especially when requests are closed without access.

Findings

What the data shows

1. Upper Manhattan waits longer than similar requests citywide. It is the slow cluster that survives correction for chance under both methods (East Harlem, Harlem, Manhattanville-West Harlem). The machine learning method also flags East New York-New Lots in Brooklyn.
2. East Harlem (North) stands out most. Requests wait a median of 0.59 days longer than similar requests, and 65% are slower than the citywide norm. The gap holds (+0.61 days) after removing its 10 busiest buildings, so it is not driven by a few problem properties. Plumbing requests wait 2.3 days longer.
3. About 1 in 4 unique requests is closed as "no access". The share is highest in lower Manhattan: 51% in Chinatown-Two Bridges, around 40% in the East Village, Lower East Side and Greenwich Village.
4. 29% of requests are repeat reports. When heat fails, many tenants in the same building call on the same day.

Impact and next steps

A monthly early-warning view for 311

Who should use this tool?
Tenants in neighborhoods that wait longer; agency managers deciding where to send inspectors; council members and community boards asking for accountability.
How it would be used?
Flag neighborhoods whose gap is large and persistent, then check staffing, routing and "no access" practices there first.
How it is maintained?
Built entirely on open data and open-source Python. Re-running one script each month refreshes every number on this page.

Next steps

  • Build an automated warning feature that can send notifications to relevant Departments when a complaint's waiting time is longer than usual.
  • Add construction and building-safety complaints (Department of Buildings).
  • Track whether gaps close over time after an intervention.
